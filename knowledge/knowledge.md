# Attachment Compass — Master Knowledge Document: Avoidant Attachment, Breakups & Reconnection

This document merges two sources. **Source A** is *Guided Awareness*, a practicing therapist and psychology doctoral student, distilled from 34 videos on avoidant attachment inside relationships; her framing is therapeutic and explicitly anti-manipulation. **Source B** is *Chris Seiter*, a breakup-recovery coach of 12+ years, distilled from 18 videos; his framing is strategic and outcome-oriented, and his primary territory is the period after a breakup.

They agree more than they disagree. Where they agree independently, that is the most trustworthy material here and is marked **Both sources agree.** Where they genuinely conflict, the conflict is preserved rather than blended, under **Where the sources differ.** Neither source is treated as the house position, and reconciliation is never assumed to be your goal.

This is a general guide, not therapy, not diagnosis, and not one-size-fits-all — every avoidant is different; the only guaranteed similarity is the attachment style itself.

## 0. SAFETY GATE — read before any pattern analysis

**This gate fires on every retrieval, not only when the user asks about it.** It comes before the core model on purpose: nothing further down this document may be used until this check has been run against what the person is actually describing.

**Hard rule.** If the person describes any of the following — physical violence or being hit, threats of harm, coercive control, stalking or being followed or monitored, non-consensual sexual pressure or coerced sex, isolation from money, friends, family or transport, having their documents or finances withheld, or simply being afraid of their partner — then:

1. **Name it plainly.** Say what it is. Do not soften it, do not hedge it, do not fold it into a paragraph about attachment.
2. **Stop the attachment framing entirely for that turn.** Do not explain the behavior as deactivation, a core wound, a test, a pain gauntlet, or a phase. Attachment language must never be used to explain, soften, contextualize, or predict abuse. "He's avoidant" is not an account of why someone was hurt.
3. **Route to professional and emergency support before offering any pattern content**, and only return to pattern content if the person, after that, explicitly asks for it and there is no active danger.

**Resources (US).** Immediate danger: 911. Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text **988**. National Domestic Violence Hotline: **1-800-799-7233**, or text **START** to **88788** (thehotline.org has a live chat and a quick-exit button). Sexual assault: RAINN, **1-800-656-4673**. Outside the US, route to the local emergency number and the nearest national domestic-violence or crisis line; say plainly that you are giving a US-centric list and ask what country they are in.

**Self-harm and suicide statements — from anyone, about anyone.** If the person describes their own thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or their partner's, treat it as real and urgent regardless of anyone's read of the motive, including in situations this document elsewhere describes as manipulation (see the narcissism material in Section 5). It is not the user's job to determine whether a statement about self-harm is sincere or a lever. Route to 988 or emergency services and do not analyze the relationship pattern in that turn.

**Why this section exists.** The rest of this document touches abuse repeatedly — "borderline abusive" testing (1.6, 2.10, 5), stalking and legal notices as manipulation tactics (5), a domestic-abuse-shelter statistic (5), "never tolerate mistreatment" (5) — and every one of those passages is written as *pattern analysis*. None of it is a substitute for this gate. A user in danger gets this section, not those.

**One thing this gate does not do:** it does not tell anyone they must leave, and it does not treat repeated returns as failure. The shelter statistic in Section 5 — a woman leaves an abusive relationship an average of seven times before leaving for good — belongs here too, for exactly that reason.

## 1. CORE MODEL

### 1.1 The wound and where it comes from
Avoidant attachment forms in childhood: emotions were treated as wrong, toughness and independence were demanded, and love was only secured by shutting feelings down (e.g., a parent who pulled away whenever the child showed emotion — effectively "I can't love you if you're too emotional"). Usefulness was valued instead of emotions, so acts of service became their love language. Many had strict parents where nothing was ever enough — fail and the parent is disappointed, succeed and approval is withheld — which is why avoidants can't self-acknowledge and become obsessed with work and money as visible proof "I am valuable." Many were actually very loving young children who were shunned for "caring too much," so when they finally attach, they love very strongly. Because the wound formed in infancy, their personality was shaped with it — "there is no person underneath that"; traits like hyper-independence may always remain (compromise territory), but the fear itself can shrink dramatically.

Seiter arrives at a near-identical origin story from a different angle: caregivers who supplied material needs but not emotional attunement, or who actively dismissed the child's feelings (telling a boy not to cry), often because their own vulnerability disgusted them — so the child learns to associate their own vulnerable feelings with disgust and handles emotions alone. He adds a developmental frame borrowed from clinical social worker Erica Kazar: infants start in rough water and are carried to calm water only by a caregiver who co-regulates them moment to moment, with felt safety internalized by roughly age three. The avoidant, in this picture, was never carried across; self-soothing became the rehearsed habit. He also cites research putting roughly 45% of the variability in anxious attachment and 39% in avoidant attachment down to genetics — meaning most of it is circumstance, not wiring.

**Core-wound framing (Seiter's organizing device).** Every insecure style has one master wound that triggers all its coping. The anxious person's is abandonment and loss; the dismissive avoidant's is losing independence; the fearful avoidant carries both at once. Secure attachment has none. His one-line decoder for confusing avoidant behavior: ask whether the behavior buys them more independence. If it does, that's the motive. This maps closely onto the therapist's account of what sits *inside* commitment fear (see 1.2) and is a useful compression of it — but treat it as a lens, not a law; the therapist's version is broader and includes fears of judgment, humiliation, and losing self-trust that don't reduce cleanly to independence.

Subtypes: **fearful avoidants (FA)** score highest on adverse childhood experiences, had to alternate between expressive and detached depending on a volatile caregiver, run hot-and-cold, and stay in unsatisfying relationships longer; **dismissive avoidants (DA)** learned detachment earns parental love, commit very slowly but then stick to one person. Seiter makes the same insistence on subtype: assuming every avoidant is dismissive avoidant is, in his phrasing, like assuming every dog is a Labrador — and he discloses that he himself tested as dismissive avoidant in 2020. Trust template: anxious people trust others but not themselves; FAs trust neither; DAs trust themselves too much and others basically not at all.

**Both sources agree** that avoidants genuinely want love and connection — they are not indifferent people. The therapist: avoidants desire love but struggle to express it, lie out of shame or conflict-avoidance rather than for gain, and blame themselves ("I'm the fault, don't stay with me") rather than playing victim. **She draws a firm line from this: avoidance is not narcissism — see the differ note at the end of Section 5 for her behavioral test and where Seiter parts company.** Seiter disputes the common claim that avoidants don't want anything serious: in his read they want an idealized version of it badly, and the wanting is what childhood turned into a defense. He also reads the wider avoidant online community as full of people afraid of being alone forever while believing they can't change — the opposite of indifference.

### 1.2 Deactivation and the internal conflict
Deactivation strategies mute the avoidant's feelings — they set the symptoms of love dormant; it does not mean they're out of love. Deactivation triggers when emotions run high — which includes both more fighting AND more love/closeness, because subconsciously love equates to distance (that's how they secured parental love). Their commitment-fear doesn't feel like fear to them — it feels like refusal, stubbornness, "I just don't want to." They're not afraid of commitment itself but of what's *inside* it: losing space, autonomy, and identity; being controlled, manipulated, humiliated, judged; making the wrong decision and losing trust in themselves. The risks are real, but the ratio is off — they expect a 9 or 10 magnitude when it's really a 1. Detachment often has nothing to do with the partner (work stress, family issues); avoidants struggle to handle multiple things at once. They can leave people they love: they bond to the overall *feeling* around a person, so if they feel triggered, anxious, and cornered in the moment, they may go even while loving you.

Seiter contributes a concrete inventory of what deactivation looks like from outside, drawn from the book *Attached*: saying or thinking they're not ready to commit, fixating on small flaws, pining after a previous partner, flirting with others, withholding "I love you," pulling back exactly when things are going well, pursuing relationships with no possible future, checking out mentally, keeping secrets, being deliberately vague, and avoiding physical closeness. He also cites clinical psychologist Dr. John Paul Garrison for the point that avoidance is an anxiety-management system, not the absence of anxiety — which is why avoidants can be quiet control freaks about the relationship's pace and about who reaches out first. This lines up with the therapist's observation that almost all insecurely attached people have some form of control issue, and that avoidants prefer to control the relationship's pace out of fear of being controlled and suffocated.

**The commitment ladder and the personal trigger threshold (Seiter).** His own claimed contribution to attachment theory — he credits Bowlby, Ainsworth and Main for the theory itself — is that avoidant breakups cluster at identifiable *tipping points*: being asked on a date, romantic texting, romantic phone calls, becoming official, meeting parents, getting a pet together, discussing moving in, actually moving in, serious marriage talk, ring shopping, engagement, marriage, buying a house, having a child. Each rung demands a new tier of intimacy and reads as an assault on independence. Every avoidant has a personal threshold somewhere on that ladder where the alarm goes off, and thresholds differ. In his flagship case, the ex left roughly three months after moving into a house together, about two and a half years into the relationship. He also observed a miniature version in his own coaching: texting an ex goes fine, but text→phone call, or phone call→in person, hits a wall, because each medium demands more real-time exposure and offers less control over pacing.

His pacing model for this is the boiling-frog parable — which he explicitly flags as not literally true biologically but useful as a model: avoidants bolt from sudden temperature changes and tolerate gradual ones, so *where* someone's threshold sits often just reflects how abruptly the heat was raised. **Both sources agree on the underlying point** from opposite vocabularies: the therapist's "accepting their timeline *is* accepting them" and Seiter's "turn the heat up slowly" are the same instruction. That convergence — one arrived at through clinical work with avoidant patients, the other through breakup post-mortems — makes pacing one of the most reliable claims in this document.

**The battery model (Seiter).** In an anxious-avoidant pairing, each partner's battery is charged by a different thing: the avoidant recharges through space and preserved independence, the anxious partner through reassurance. Once the honeymoon ends, the avoidant's battery drains first and they start nitpicking; the nitpicking drains the anxious partner, who seeks reassurance, which drains the avoidant further. Both end up empty. This is a mechanical restatement of the therapist's "box" model (2.1) and of her observation that reactions like irritation are investment — the nitpicking phase is the battery reading low, not the love ending.

**Stability-vs-mystery drift (Seiter, flagged by him as personal theory).** People want both stability and mystery; the two are opposites, every relationship starts at some ratio, and the ratio drifts toward stability over time. When it tips far enough, the avoidant feels this isn't what they signed up for. He decodes ex-complaints like "boring," "too stable," "complacent" this way, and speculates that some avoidants disengage once they feel there are no layers left to discover. He tells viewers to take this one with a grain of salt, and it should be held loosely: it is his opinion, and the therapist's material offers a competing read of the same complaint (that closeness itself, not familiarity, is the trigger — see 2.1).

**Phantom ex (Seiter, from *Attached*).** After devaluing a real relationship, an avoidant may later idealize a past partner — forgetting everything that annoyed them and longing for a lost love. Offered a choice between a real person asking for commitment and a fantasy person asking for nothing, they take the fantasy: it's unattainable, so it protects their independence and supplies a permanent reason to keep the wall up. He calls it the ultimate deactivation strategy. His own extension — labelled as his theory — is that the phantom is often a *composite*, best qualities harvested from several past partners and stitched together into an ideal no living person can match; it may even be an ideal or a fear of missing out rather than a real person, and an avoidant may hold several tied to different life stages. Practically: an avoidant who keeps measuring you against an ex or an unreachable standard is deactivating, not evaluating.

### 1.3 Shame, guilt, and failure
Failure is one of the avoidant's biggest triggers. After running from a relationship, their dominant feeling is: "relationships are hard, I made a mistake, you're never going to forgive me." Unhealed avoidants handle guilt by avoiding the topic or getting irritated (they're shutting down the "noise" of having disappointed you — "it's not you, but it does come out at you"), downplaying ("you're exaggerating" — denial precisely because it feels bad), making excuses, breaking promises to change once the guilt fades (more FA), contradicting or rewriting your account of what happened (more FA), or — if the guilt is unmanageable — breaking up with you to escape it (more DA). On that last-but-one item specifically: whatever is driving it — shame, conflict-avoidance, or something else — being routinely told your memory of events is wrong is corrosive, and if it is persistent it is worth taking to a therapist rather than resolving inside an attachment frame. Describe what happened; don't use "gaslighting" as a diagnosis of the person. Healing avoidants apologize genuinely without theatrics, explain for clarity while staying open to questions, show gradual visible change that doesn't revert, ask to be held accountable ("if you notice I haven't done it, tell me"), and later seek recognition of their improvement — which is itself a form of commitment. Many who "discard" genuinely believed disappearing would hurt you less than staying.

Seiter's crowdsourced version corroborates the motive set from the avoidants' own mouths. Polling an avoidant-attachment subreddit of roughly 29,000 self-identified members, he found ghosters' stated reasons clustered into five themes: dodging emotional discomfort or conflict, feeling swamped by the other person's attachment or expectations, believing the other person deserves better, low self-worth and fear of not meeting the partner's needs, and depression or low tolerance for social contact. Three of those five are the therapist's "I'll hurt you less by leaving" and "I'm the fault" almost verbatim, arrived at independently. He treats the stated reasons with some skepticism and suspects a deeper driver — fear of being permanently alone, paired with a belief they can't change it. He also flags his own evidence as weak: 19 self-selected Reddit responses is not research, and he went there only because avoidant-specific breakup studies barely exist.

### 1.4 Bond vs chemistry
Passionate love is fast, electric, chemical — and it's the part avoidants can fake: many put on a charming, romantic front early precisely because they *don't* feel deep attachment yet (and are ashamed they struggle with love). Compassionate love — slower, steadier, consistent — is what "deep genuine love" means here, and it's the stage avoidants most value yet most tend to sabotage, because when authentic love starts, the fear starts. Genuine bond is different from chemistry: it's how you *know* each other — knowing how they act in a bad mood, small repair attempts after hurting you, giving each other space. Many avoidants fall in love because they feel you know something about them they never said in words. Depth can't be forced with anyone — they notice its absence when comparing conversations with others to conversations with you. Warning: fast early intimacy, promises, and commitment followed by collapse usually means they *don't* love you — a loving avoidant charms early but never commits early.

Seiter's "together but apart" adds a second failure mode on the other side of the same distinction: an avoidant can be perfectly consistent about *making time* while the time itself stays emotionally flat. Presence is not connection. **Both sources agree** that the diagnostic question is depth, not frequency or reliability of contact — the therapist through bond-vs-chemistry, Seiter through quality-vs-quantity of time.

### 1.5 How avoidants fall in love
Avoidants vet slowly — like they buy houses and cars: long research, slow decision, then ride-or-die. Romance is a checklist to them (some literally state requirements on first dates); sudden commitment after years usually means the checklist quietly got fulfilled. They don't waste time on anyone who isn't valuable to them — "if an avoidant wants to give you breadcrumbs, they just might not even be there." They take action seriously, not words: love shows up as acts of service (changing your tire, airport pickups, packed meals), teaching you their hobbies, building you into their regimented schedule (if you're in their schedule, "you're pretty much here to stay"), consistency and reliability, silent sacrifices of comfort they never expect recognized, and blunt honesty. When truly attached they develop standards and expectations of you — this is reliance and vulnerability, not flaw-finding. "Crashing out" with "you don't know how much I tolerate for you" is a badly-worded request for appreciation. They carry the concept of "the one" more than most styles; once committed they give their all — and caring how you view them is, to an avoidant, admitting they need you. They often don't know they love you until much later — sometimes too late.

Seiter's additions here are about *how affection gets disguised on the way out of them*:

- **Micro-gifting** — a term he coins by analogy to micro-cheating. Avoidants give small, obscure, deliberately downplayed gifts and minimize their own gesture so it can't be rejected. Three types he names: the found-object gift (a rock, coin, or trinket presented as something they stumbled across), the practical-yet-impersonal gift (a pen, notebook, keychain, framed as a spare), and the I-got-it-for-free gift (a promo item offered as worthless). His own example: framing hard-won rodeo tickets as free so rejection wouldn't sting. He also notes avoidants *receive* gifts strangely — humoring the giver, then not using the item, losing or breaking it, quietly giving it away, or buying the same thing for themselves and using that one.
- **Intellectual sparring as flirtation** — arguing a position they don't hold, playful teasing, intense debate. Connection without emotional exposure, and sometimes covert respect for your mind. He warns it can equally be the setup for a "something's missing" exit, so read which one you're in.
- **Virgin ground** — his term for a disclosure they have never given anyone else. He calls it the single strongest sign on any list, because it means the avoidant acted directly against their own core wound.

**Seiter rates this the strongest single sign; the therapist treats never-before-made disclosure as major vulnerability but reserves "the most important sign" for staying and continuing to attempt communication through hard stretches.** Her version of the disclosure marker is sharing shameful past, day-to-day stress with no ask, and small mundane details — "major vulnerability for an avoidant." Seiter's is virgin ground. Both independently identify never-before-made disclosure as a major marker, from clinical practice and from coaching case review respectively; they simply rank it differently, and both rankings are preserved in the Signs dictionary. **Both also agree** that avoidant love is read through behavior rather than surface message, and that minimizing language ("it's nothing," "I just found it," "this is casual") should not be taken at face value.

### 1.6 Testing behavior
Avoidants test largely subconsciously — it's maladaptive coping, and unlike anxious testing (which seeks reactions to validate insecurity), avoidant testing is *vetting*: "are you valuable enough to invest in?" Concrete forms: saying no on purpose to see if you'll accept disappointment respectfully (the sweet spot is disappointed-but-respectful — many decide "she's the one" when you pass); declaring "I'll never get married / I can't give you what you want" (both a back-off move and a test — many later do exactly those things); deliberately delaying replies during good stretches (unhealthy — what they actually want is reassurance they're too proud to request); blunt criticism ("I don't enjoy your cooking") to see whether you'll judge and force them or hear them; offering feedback to gauge whether you can take it; and sharing insecurities to test whether you'll side with them. When they come back after a breakup, expect them to be assessing whether it is safe to re-engage. You do not have to counter-test — but not testing is not the same as not evaluating, and it is not the same as having no requirements. Name what you need plainly (2.9). If passing their assessment requires you to have no stated needs at all, that is the gauntlet, not vetting.

**How to respond when tested:** To "I can't give you what you want," answer with acceptance plus agency: "I know what I want. If I don't like you, I'll tell you and leave. You don't need to make that decision for me. You're not as bad as you think you are." If insecurity flares while they're away, raise it only *after* the trip, self-located: "I was feeling insecure and need reassurance — I do believe you're loyal." When they offer blunt feedback, hear it out without denial. When they share a family or childhood insecurity, explicitly side with them.

Seiter documents overlapping behavior but reads it far more harshly. His three recurring tests are: the **pain gauntlet** (withdrawn affection, inconsistency, cancelled plans, going quiet, subtle criticism, all while watching how you react — rooted, via clinical psychologist Hal Shorey, in inconsistent early caregiving, and aimed unconsciously at confirming that anyone who gets close eventually leaves); the **phantom ex perfection test** (being measured against a composite ideal you cannot match); and the **endless repeat** — passing both doesn't end the process, the cycle simply resets. His conclusion: trust for an avoidant isn't earned in a single moment of loyalty, and by the time you've proven it enough times, the testing has usually ground the relationship down.

> **Where the sources differ — is testing a hurdle to pass, or a red flag to leave over?**
>
> **The therapist's position:** testing is subconscious vetting from someone deciding whether you're safe to invest in. Passing it is often the moment they decide you're the one, and she supplies specific, non-manipulative scripts for each form. Her frame: this is a wound expressing itself clumsily, and skilled responses shrink it.
>
> **Seiter's position:** he calls the pain gauntlet "not normal" and "borderline abusive," and says explicitly not to treat it as a challenge to conquer. He names the optimistic reinterpretation — "their coldness proves how much they care" — as the trap that keeps people stuck, and warns that avoidant content online feeds "fantasy bonds" where readers decide they'll be the exception and endure indefinitely.
>
> **What would make one fit your case better:** Seiter himself supplies the dividing line, and it happens to be the same one the therapist's material implies. Inside an established, committed relationship where the person is otherwise consistent and showing gradual change, there is room to work through testing behavior — that's the therapist's territory, and her scripts are the better tool. In the *early, uncommitted* stage of dating, before any real commitment exists, Seiter advises against trying to work through it at all. Two further discriminators: whether the tests are *shrinking* over time (therapist's "measure against their own baseline six months ago") — if they're not shrinking after real time and real effort, Seiter's read is the accurate one; and whether the testing includes contempt, engineered instability, or manufactured conflict, which sits outside the vetting frame entirely. Endurance is not a strategy in either source's world.

### 1.7 Placeholder vs "the one"
The opposite of love is not hate — it's indifference. Reactions, even negative ones (irritation, anger, shutting down, blame, arguments, endless expectations), are emotional investment; total nonchalance where nothing gets a rise out of them is the placeholder signal. If you're the one: they have lots of expectations of you; they ask for recognition ("do you not see how much I do for you?"); they know your moods, preferences, and standing orders in detail; their actions are stronger than their words (they resist verbal promises because breaking one means failing you, but real progress shows); and they slowly open up over time. Placeholder: no expectations, no recognition-seeking, forgets details a colleague would know, promises marriage/kids freely with zero action, lies just to not rock the boat, and no momentum. The one-year observation — treat it as the therapist's clinical impression, not a rule: avoidants rarely stay a year with someone they don't care about, since they'd rather be alone. That makes duration weak evidence *against* placeholder status, and no evidence at all about whether the relationship is good for you. 2.10's counterpart applies with equal force: a year with no movement is itself a finding. Caveat: FAs may lie to spare your feelings *because* they love you, so the honesty test doesn't work on them.

Seiter's tipping-point test is a clean behavioral addition to this list: when an avoidant genuinely loves you, the commitment milestones stop detonating the relationship. They may still withdraw somewhat at each rung, but not enough to end it. A milestone that produces mild withdrawal rather than an exit is real evidence of attachment. He offers a second: withholding "I love you" behind an impossibly high bar (one Reddit avoidant's criteria amounted to would-take-a-bullet, would-hide-a-body levels of loyalty) is a deactivation strategy, not a standard to try to meet — so absence of the words, alongside clearly loving behavior, does not put you in placeholder territory.

He also flags a genuinely dark pattern the therapist's material doesn't cover: some avoidants quietly set up a *backup relationship* — a new connection forming in early stages while still with you, not necessarily cheating, but pre-securing a landing spot so they can never be the one left with nothing. Where the therapist reads "flirts with others" as often meaning no love, Seiter reads it as rejection-proofing. Either way, treat it as serious information rather than as a quirk.

### 1.8 What they need to feel safe
Acceptance is a core desire — accepting their timeline *is* accepting them. They need: non-judgmental space (they're very sensitive to judgment); respect (no eye-rolling, sarcasm, mocking, comparing — rule of thumb: if you wouldn't text it to your boss, be careful texting it to your partner); consistency (not high text volume — knowing you're there); built-in space that never fully blurs; a partner who manages their own emotions at least some of the time; reassurance about their *specific* fears (manipulation, control, punishment for rescheduling — not "I'll never leave you"); appreciation of effort over outcome; a partner who lets go of little things; equals, not a pedestal; and a partner with real self-esteem — flexible like a rubber band, not rigid ultimatums. Once they feel accepted with their original stance, they become far more willing to compromise — non-acceptance is the main barrier to avoidants changing.

**Both sources agree** space is essential and that the avoidant often cannot articulate why it works — **but they rank it differently:** Seiter calls space the most attractive thing on offer, because it refills the battery the relationship was draining, while the therapist puts *acceptance* first and treats built-in space that never fully blurs, plus consistency, as one need among roughly ten — and names self-esteem building as the single variable that makes an avoidant lean in (2.9). That is a genuine difference in what each treats as the primary lever, and it is worth preserving rather than merging. **Both also agree** that the avoidant's worst-case scenario is being flooded by a partner's anxiety and having to supply constant reassurance — the therapist through her "full box feels suffocating" model, Seiter directly.

Seiter adds a counterintuitive note on love languages, flagged by him as thin evidence: intuition says acts of service (practical, no emotional exposure required), but he cites a therapist-run site claiming dismissive avoidants tend to prize *quality time and physical touch* — the languages requiring the least verbal expression while still conveying affection — backed by a Reddit thread where 4 of 11 avoidants unprompted named those two. He acknowledges the sample is small. This sits in tension with the therapist's acts-of-service account; the honest synthesis is that avoidants tend to prefer whichever channel carries affection without requiring them to say anything, and which channel that is varies by person. Ask yours.

### 2.1 Pulling away / deactivation
**What's happening:** Usually not about you — it's childhood (they may even blame you and be wrong; "nobody knows themselves that well"). Closeness itself is their subconscious cue to shut down; pulling away after milestones often happens while feelings are actually *deepening*. Often it's external stress they won't show. The "box" model: you fill the relationship box halfway waiting for them to match — but a full box feels heavy and suffocating to them. Seiter's parallel reading: withdrawal that fires precisely at a commitment milestone means you've hit their rung on the ladder, and a sharp reaction to an abrupt escalation signals they detected a fast temperature change — not that they reject the destination.

**DO:** Become a little more independent (job, health, hobbies, friends); drop your "box" contribution to a quarter, let them fill a quarter, then build 10% at a time; communicate directly and logically ("You're canceling a lot, I don't like it — what can we do together to make this stop?"); recognize their acts of service as bids for connection; believe them when they say you didn't do anything.
**DON'T:** Blame yourself; smother with your own bids (frequent compliments feel insincere and suffocating); read reduced replies as loss of love; interrogate mid-withdrawal.
**Expect:** With more space plus consistency, they typically re-approach — sometimes volunteering steps forward within weeks. Slow rebuild, not overnight.

**Ethics note on those first two DO items** (same treatment as the contrast effect in 4.4 — keep the mechanism, restate the motive): build the independent parts of your life because a life that is only this relationship is a bad life for you regardless of what they do; the fact that it speaks their love-language of space is a side effect, not the reason. And read the box model as a description of what suffocates them, not a dial you turn to extract reciprocity — if you'd re-flood the box the moment they responded, you were measuring output, not changing how you relate.

**Intimacy withdrawal:** Pulling away from physical intimacy is its own recurring case — a triggered fear response, "nothing to do with you," not lost attraction. Desire typically returns once they feel safe again, but it can take a year or more of patience. A workable tactic: verbally agree together to take breaks from high-pressure topics like bedroom intimacy — the agreed pause removes the pressure driving the shutdown. **But: a year is an estimate of what is possible, not a term you owe anyone.** You are allowed to decide that a sexless or intimacy-withdrawn relationship does not work for you at any point, without a diagnostic threshold and without waiting for a deadline to expire. Withdrawal of intimacy can also mean things other than deactivation — another relationship, a medical issue, a question about orientation, or simply your own consent, which is yours to withhold at any point too. ### 2.2 Shutdown mid-conflict / stonewalling
**What's happening:** They weren't modeled how to process extreme feelings; your high emotion floods them and they can't hear anything. Continuing last night's argument today reads as a *second* fight. FAs may go silent for days. The emotion you see — irritation, anger, shutting down — is investment, not indifference. Seiter defines stonewalling the same way (withdrawing, shutting down, physiologically flooded) and adds that the trigger is specifically a perceived threat to independence, so the withdrawal is a defense, not proof of disinterest.

**DO (therapist):** Stay grounded; lead with logic that includes feeling; take accountability for your side *during* the argument; shelve non-pressing issues ("our relationship is overall good — back burner, let's do something fun"); bounce back the next day; frame it "me and you against the problem."
**DON'T:** Chase, corner, or give ultimatums; drag the fight across days; hold arguments against them later; treat their next-day normalcy as not caring.
**Expect:** They often quietly start doing the disputed thing a couple weeks after the fight simmers down — delayed change is more permanent than immediate change.

> **Where the sources differ — during a shutdown, do you speak or do you go quiet?**
>
> **The therapist's position:** confident, direct communication is the only language avoidants actually hear. Pouting, hinting and withdrawing mean nothing to them, and prolonged confusion can push them out even when they care. Her instruction is to say the thing plainly and without ultimatum, and specifically *not* to silently stew or act hurt instead of speaking.
>
> **Seiter's position:** "when they pull back, you pull back" is his central mantra. Withdrawal is the avoidant signalling they don't want to solve or talk right now, so match it with space. He tells anxious partners to stop hunting for a way to make the stonewalling stop, and to ask what a secure person would do — a secure person gives space because they have other things worth doing.
>
> **These are not reconcilable by splitting the difference, but they are reconcilable by sequencing, which is close to what each source does elsewhere.** Seiter's own advice is that after the space period ends you *do* reconnect verbally, using tactical empathy and labelling — naming their unspoken fear out loud, calmly. The therapist's advice is that you raise the issue when the person can hear it, not mid-flood, and that you leave non-pressing issues on the back burner. So: space *during* the flood, words *after* it. What tips you toward one source: if you have been speaking clearly and repeatedly and the relationship keeps cycling, Seiter's read (you are supplying the pursuit that keeps their defenses up) is more likely correct. If you have been silently absorbing, hinting, and hoping they notice, the therapist's read (they cannot hear anything you haven't said outright) is more likely correct. And note her hard limit: silence used as a message is a losing move with avoidants. (Seiter's silence is time-boxed space, not a message — he treats the pattern-break itself as the active ingredient.)

**Seiter's SPACE calibration formula** (for how much space, when you've chosen the space route) — severity sets a baseline: mild stonewalling 1–2 days, moderate 3–5 days, severe a week or more. Then adjust: frequent past stonewalling +1–2 days, rare −1–2; a low need for space −1 day, high +1; an immediate reply to your contact attempt −1 day, no reply +1; a calm climate −1 day, tense or confrontational +1. His worked example: moderate (4) + frequent (+2) + high need (+1) + no response (+1) + neutral climate (0) ≈ 8 days. He is explicit that there is such a thing as too much space, citing a viewer's two months of silence as genuinely excessive — which is why he built a formula rather than recommending blanket distance. Seiter also imports two negotiation techniques from Chris Voss — *tactical empathy* (grasping what the other person feels and what sits underneath it) and *labelling* (naming their emotion aloud and reflecting it back), citing a Harlem standoff resolved after roughly six hours of repeated labelling. **Ethics note:** he acknowledges the tension himself — these come from a hostage negotiator's playbook and are aimed at increasing your influence over what happens next. ### 2.3 The breakup itself
**What's happening (therapist):** Avoidants grieve the right person in stages: (1) numbness/detachment/even excitement ("I can finally be independent" — deactivation, not indifference; DAs stay here longer), (2) shock, (3) denial ("I never loved you" — protecting the belief that your emotions can't control them), (4) anger/distraction (FAs rebound into relationships they don't care about; DAs take trips, plan moves), (5) bargaining ("how are you?", "I'm sorry", "I miss you" — heavy hidden self-blame), (6) deep grief — the longest stage, never shown to you (DAs can silently stay here for years), (7) reflection, (8) acceptance, (9) growth. The initial "freedom" fades into emptiness — independence stops tasting good. FAs feel the pain sooner; DAs take much longer to register it.

**Hold this stage model exactly as loosely as 4.2 asks you to hold Seiter's five stages.** This is the therapist's model from her own practice, not an established sequence. People skip stages, run them out of order, show only one, or show none. Nothing here is a clock, and a stage you cannot observe — stage six in particular — is not evidence that it is happening.

**Both sources agree, strongly and independently, on the most misread moment of a breakup:** the avoidant's early relief is not indifference. The therapist calls it stage-one deactivation; Seiter calls it **separation elation** — stage six of his eight-stage "avoidant death wheel," which he places directly opposite the honeymoon on the wheel by design and describes as a second, smaller honeymoon: the first is love without strings, the second is the joy of getting independence back. **Both also agree** that a fast rebound is not proof they've moved on. This is the single most consistently corroborated claim in the post-breakup material, and it should be treated as reliable.

Seiter's death wheel in full: (1) wanting to be loved, (2) finding someone and entering a honeymoon, (3) core wound triggered — usually by the partner's anxious core wound, (4) thinking about leaving, (5) leaving, (6) separation elation, (7) the breakup finally landing, (8) depression and "why does this always happen to me," then back to stage one. He runs a parallel anxious wheel that matches through stage two, then diverges: instead of noticing warning signs, the anxious person becomes codependent and over-focused, doubles down when they sense a breakup coming, gets left, and throws everything at winning the avoidant back — which is where he says most of his audience is stuck.

**DO:** Be yourself — including honestly admitting once, directly and respectfully, that you still want it ("I understand you don't want to be together. **DON'T:** Play games or perform indifference — they know you care, and pretending reads as inauthentic; beg or repeat your case; take the happy/detached phase at face value.
**Expect:** If they discarded you over an external struggle, they'll very likely reach out once it resolves. Physical reminders of you in their space intensify the craving; suppressing the missing makes it stronger.

### 2.6 Friendzone / "we're just friends"

### 4.0 Deciding whether you even want them back
This is where Seiter is at his best and least strategic, and it belongs before any tactic — which is why it now sits first.

**His two questions, in order:** *Why do I want them back* — love, fear of being alone, social pressure, or something else? And *are they actually worth getting back?* He says the reason matters more than the tactic, because a bad reason makes any tactic pointless, and that if you can't answer both well, your time is better spent elsewhere. He explicitly declines to give a satisfying blanket answer and says the space itself is what should produce the clarity.

**Knock them off the pedestal first** — not by demonizing them or feeling superior, but by honestly recognizing they have flaws and limits, and that the idealized version in your head may not match the real person. Only then re-ask the two questions. His stated ideal outcome is reaching the point where you genuinely realize you don't need them back.

**Understand what you're feeling.** He cites Helen Fisher's brain-imaging work on recently rejected people who still loved their exes: looking at photos of the rejector lit up regions tied to craving, reward, motivation, physical pain and distress, far more than photos of neutral acquaintances. Rejection intensifies the pull rather than killing it. He also uses Hofer's rat-pup separation research — separated pups showed drops in activity, heart rate and growth hormone, and substituting individual maternal functions fixed only one symptom each; only reunion fixed everything at once — as the mechanism behind why reuniting *feels* like the only thing that will resolve all the distress simultaneously. Read your withdrawal symptoms as evidence of an attachment system in distress, not as proof this person is your soulmate.

**Related distortion to check for:** *Attached*'s account of protest behavior, which Seiter relays — after conflict, the anxious partner floods with negative emotion, then calms and floods with good memories and regret and reaches out, while the avoidant does the reverse, shutting off attachment memories and recalling the partner's worst. Immediately after a fight, the two of you are remembering completely different relationships. Neither memory is the accurate one.

### 4.1 What no contact is actually for — and what it is not
> **Precondition:** before using anything below, the two questions in 4.0 have to have real answers, and the do-not-pursue conditions there override every tactic in this section.

Seiter's own most consistent position, stated across several videos, is that no contact is for *getting over your ex*, not for getting them back. He is direct that doing it as a manipulation to summon someone tends to fail, and that the version he endorses treats reconciliation as a possible side effect rather than the objective. He gives a second, independent reason that has nothing to do with the ex: chronically breaking no contact feeds a spiral of negative self-talk — feeling permanently alone, concluding you are the problem — so the rule protects you regardless of outcome.

What it is *not*: a program of permanent silence. He pushes back on his own audience here — at some point, if you want anything, you have to actually talk to the person, and silence alone accomplishes nothing. And it is not a lever that reliably produces contact: in his own community poll, roughly two-thirds of respondents said their ex never reached out at all during no contact, and most of those respondents were the ones who had been dumped.

**Why space changes what an avoidant feels — and why that is not a reason to do it (Seiter's mechanism).** Anxious people rush to fix things, which keeps the avoidant's defenses firing. Remove the interaction and the deactivation system has nothing to react to, so suppressed feelings can surface. He adds the **peak-end rule**: people remember an experience by its peak moments and its ending. Without space, an avoidant's memory of you is dominated by the negative ending; with enough nonthreatening distance, they start reaching for the peaks. He names two conditions that must *both* hold for an avoidant to return: they have to actually be left alone, **and** they have to discover the dating pool without you is disappointing. If either fails, the mechanism doesn't fire. **Ethics note:** recorded accurately, this is a mechanism, not a lever — neither condition is something you can produce, and treating no contact as a countdown to them meeting the second one is the exact failure mode this subsection opens by warning against. If you would abandon the silence the day you learned it wasn't working on them, you are not in no contact, you are in a waiting room.

**Lengths he gives** (his general practice, not rules): three viable windows — 21, 30, and 45 days, with 30 the most common. If it's your first attempt, go longer rather than shorter. His own preferred structure is flexible: commit to 45 days spent working on getting over the ex, then honestly assess — if you're genuinely over them you can consider reaching out; if not, extend another 45. Therapist Julia Christina, interviewed on his channel, recommends 90 days, comparing it to a probationary period at a new job.

### 4.2 What typically happens on their side during it
**Everything in this subsection is Seiter's general pattern from his coaching practice, not a rule.** He says so himself, repeatedly and unusually clearly: the stages are an average, exes may skip them, run them out of order, experience only one, or show none at all, and every situation is unique. Read it as a map of what is *common*, and do not measure your progress against the clock.

**His five stages for a man during a 30-day no contact**, which he explicitly likens to the five stages of grief:

1. **Calm and assured** (roughly day 3 through week one) — he's on the opposite side of the emotional spectrum from you. He initiated, so he's pleased, feels the weight lifted, sees a clear path forward.
2. **Worry sets in from the silence** (roughly week one to two) — relationships establish communication patterns, habitual times of day and rhythms. Invert the pattern and he notices; the noticing becomes worry. This is where a cordial "hey, what's up" tends to appear.
3. **Anger at being ignored** (roughly week two to two and a half — around the halfway mark) — the tone flips. He reaffirms the breakup out loud and says deliberately hurtful things: glad he ended it, never loved you. Seiter's read is that this is anger at the broken pattern, not a considered position, which is why it passes.
4. **Confrontation with loss** (roughly week two and a half to three) — the anger burns off and for the first time he genuinely confronts what he lost. His original frame was that he could summon you back whenever he chose; the silence inverts that. The driver he names is fear of loss.
5. **Hope of contact** (roughly week three to the end) — having ridden the whole rollercoaster, he starts hoping you'll reach out, sometimes framed as wanting to be friends.

He notes the timings assume 30 days and would shift for the 21- or 45-day versions. **Ethics note:** he explains the sequence with an "emotional value" principle — the more emotional changes you take someone through, the more interesting you become — and advises leveraging the hope stage to capture attention. That is the part that serves you.

**Both sources agree on the most important single item in this list:** a hostile, dismissive statement made in the acute phase of a breakup is not a verdict. The therapist places "I never loved you" in her denial stage, protecting the belief that your emotions can't control them, and warns against absorbing triggered statements as final truth. Seiter places the same sentence at the halfway point of no contact and calls it anger at a broken pattern; separately, in the case that opens his commitment-ladder material, the ex said "I don't love you anymore" and "you're too much" at the breakup — and came back twice. Two sources, two methods, same conclusion: heavily discount words spoken in the trigger state.

> **Where the sources differ — how much to discount those words, and for how long.**
> The therapist's discount is bounded by her own placeholder criteria: statements are unreliable, but a year of no momentum is reliable. ### 4.3 Reaching out, and being blocked
> **Precondition:** before using anything below, the two questions in 4.0 have to have real answers, and the do-not-pursue conditions there override every tactic in this section.

**If they ask to meet up mid-no-contact:** Seiter's rule is to ignore it, with one exception — if they are explicitly saying they want you back and want to get back together, the goal has already been achieved and you can break the silence. If they ask *after* the window ends, go: he says that's usually when good things happen. His main failure case is meeting with no foundation of rapport or attraction built yet; outcomes among his clients varied, with some asking for the relationship back on the spot and others only wanting a hookup.

**Testing where you stand:** he recommends a "hook" or pattern-interrupt opener — something that breaks the expected script — and reading the *reply* as the diagnostic: a substantive answer roughly matching your message in length and meaning suggests lingering feeling; one-word replies ("cool," "that's awesome") suggest low investment. **Ethics note:** engineered openers designed to compel a response are a mild form of manipulation, and the diagnostic value doesn't require the engineering. An honest message that says something real and asks something real produces the same signal — and if a person will only respond to a clever hook and not to a straightforward message, that *is* your answer.

**What an honest opener actually looks like.** Three shapes, all built from material already in this document, none of them engineered:

1. **A specific true update, with no ask attached.** "I finished the certification last month. You were the person I kept wanting to tell, so — telling you." No question, no bid, nothing they have to do. It is a real thing, said once.
2. **A direct statement of position** — the therapist's say-it-once message from 2.3, used as the opener rather than as a closer: "I understand you don't want to be together. I still wish we could make it work, but I respect you." It resolves ambiguity in either direction and costs nothing if the answer is no.
3. **A real question about a real thing.** "I'm clearing out the hall drawer and found your charger and the blue book — do you want them, or should I drop them at your sister's?" Logistics you actually have, asked plainly, with no subtext engineered underneath.

**How to read what comes back** — this is Seiter's diagnostic with the manipulation removed, and it works on all three: a reply that roughly matches your message in length and substance is signal. A one-word reply is signal. No reply is also signal. All three are answers; none of them require you to send a second message to "check."

**Being blocked.** Seiter distinguishes a **partial block** (shut out almost everywhere, some channel still open) from a **full block** (every channel closed), and says establishing which you're in comes first. He also warns that you may have no visible indication at all: a member of his group had a friend block her, texted that friend, and the message still showed as delivered — so a delivered text is not proof you aren't blocked. Separately, from his six-signs material: exes commonly unblock on their own within about a month; a block that persists well beyond a month, or is indefinite, reads as a genuinely bad sign rather than a temporary emotional reaction.

**His unblocking tactic, recorded accurately:** he studied everyone in his community who reported being unblocked. They all said they did nothing — but on inspection, all had done something: posted content that made the ex feel on the outside looking in at a life they'd want to be part of. His playground analogy is a kid on a swing who suddenly wants nothing but to be on the full jungle gym. And his template case: a client blocked everywhere but text posted a photo she knew her ex would want to see, then texted him to go look — to see it, he had to unblock her.

**Ethics note — this one is the clearest engineering advice in Source B, and he offers no warning against it.** The tactic works by manufacturing exclusion and curiosity so that someone reverses a boundary they deliberately set. A block is a stated limit. Routing around it is a boundary violation dressed as marketing, and it is exactly the category of behavior the therapist's material rules out.

*The honest alternative that serves the same need.* The underlying need is real: you want to know whether the door is genuinely closed, and you want to stop living in ambiguity. Two things serve that without engineering anyone: (1) if a channel is genuinely open, send one clear, non-performative message stating your position and asking nothing — this is the therapist's "say it once and leave it," and it resolves the ambiguity in either direction; (2) if every channel is closed, treat the block as the answer for now. A full block is one of the clearest pieces of information you will ever get, and Seiter's own one-month benchmark says a lasting one is a bad sign. Building a life you're glad to be living is worth doing on its own terms; building it as bait is not, and it also tends not to survive contact with reality — which is precisely why his own better advice (4.0) is that the security has to be genuine.

### 4.4 Signs a breakup may not be permanent
> **Precondition:** before using anything below, the two questions in 4.0 have to have real answers, and the do-not-pursue conditions there override every tactic in this section.

Seiter built this list from patterns across his own client success stories rather than inventing it. **His primary currency is sustained investment**: how much time and energy an ex directs at you over a long stretch, not one impressive burst. A single three-hour conversation proves little; the same intensity recurring across weeks is the signal. Intermittency is normal — engaging heavily, vanishing for a few days, then returning is the ordinary rhythm, not a failure.

Two qualifiers he insists on. **Quantity without quality means nothing**: hours of daily fighting is technically investment and is not a sign the breakup is reversible. And **response quality is the tell** — replies that roughly match your message in length and substance, versus one-word answers.

**The pendulum swing:** after a breakup an ex oscillates through the whole emotional range — anger, sadness, grief, depression, sometimes elation about moving on. During the downswing into sadness they often call asking to meet in person, typically more than once. He sees this so consistently that his program pre-warns clients to expect it.

**Public distress** — sad posts, lyric posts, bitter quotes, "I'm better off without her" declarations — reads as genuine hurt even when they initiated. He cites a client whose ex recorded a rap about how much better off he was, and who later got that ex back. He also notes that men who initiate breakups often cast themselves as the victim ("look what you made me do"), which is why they can look visibly wrecked while having been the one to leave. **Ethics note on the asymmetry:** he tells clients not to post their own sadness because it makes them look bad, while treating the ex's identical posts as a favorable signal. That is optics management. The honest version of the same advice: don't broadcast grief publicly because a feed is a bad place to process pain and you will regret it — process it with people who can actually hold it.

**"Drooling from afar."** His account of how reconciliation usually happens: the ex watches from a distance while you refuse to be broken by the breakup — growing more confident, building friendships, becoming impressive. He shares a client's ex's own written account of being struck by her confidence and new life and falling for her again. His related coaching concept is the "ungettable" mindset, and to his credit he is emphatic that the pedestal has to be *earned through actual work* — exes don't start drooling over someone who followed the rules passively.

**Ethics note on the contrast effect (from his commitment-ladder video):** he also recommends creating deliberate visible change during the space — new clothes, new hair, an active social feed — because contrast can restart an avoidant's idealization, and notes one ex reacted immediately to a transformation appearing on social media. Recorded accurately, that is staging a self for an audience of one. *The honest alternative serving the same need:* the underlying need — becoming someone whose life is worth being in, independent of this relationship — is served entirely by doing it for real, and Seiter himself frames the second reason for it correctly: building a life of your own lets you take off the rose-colored glasses and actually evaluate the person. Do it for that. If it also changes how they see you, fine; if you'd stop the moment they stopped watching, you built a set, not a life.

**His timeline claims — his general pattern from his practice, not rules:** the average rebound relationship lasts about 5.2 months; most of his success stories land between month three and month six after the breakup; after a year the odds drop substantially and the standard playbook stops working. For the post-one-year case he offers "moving on without moving on" — his theory that most late reconciliations happen when the person exhausts every strategy, fails, genuinely moves on (new relationship, career breakthrough), and only then is noticed again. **Ethics note:** the phrase is self-contradictory by design and invites people to fake a state they haven't reached. His own better formulation, stated elsewhere, is the honest one: do it to get over them; the fact that this is also what tends to draw people back is an irony, not an instruction.

### 4.6 The risk of on-and-off cycles
**Both sources treat repeat cycles as the bad outcome, not the good one — and this is one of the strongest agreements in the document, because Seiter is arguing against his own commercial interest when he says it.**

### 4.7 Constrained no contact — when full separation isn't possible
**Flagged upfront: neither source addresses this case directly. Everything in this subsection is an extrapolation from their material, and it should be presented that way.** Both sources write as though clean separation is available. A large share of real breakups are not like that — a shared lease or mortgage, co-parenting or custody, the same employer or team, a tightly overlapping friend group, the same small town, a shared business.

**What the rule reduces to when contact is structurally forced.** No contact was never really about the absence of the person; both sources' actual mechanism is the absence of *relationship-processing* contact — the pursuit, the reassurance-seeking, the reopening of the ending. So when presence is unavoidable, keep the presence and cut the rest:

- **Logistics only.** Rent, bills, handover times, the kid's dentist, the client deadline. Short, factual, answerable.
- **No relationship talk.** No post-mortems, no "can we just talk about what happened," no relitigating the ending, no floating hypotheticals about getting back together.
- **No reassurance-seeking in either direction.** Not "are we okay," not "do you still think about me," not checking how they're really doing as a proxy for it.
- **Written channels where you can.** Co-parenting apps, email, shared calendars — asynchronous, on the record, and low on real-time emotional exposure. This also happens to match 1.2's channel-escalation point in reverse.
- **Decline the optional overlap.** You do not have to take the ride, the lunch, the shared errand, or the mutual-friend dinner you don't want. Attend what matters to *you*; skip what only exists to create proximity.
- **Ask friends not to relay.** No updates about them to you, no updates about you to them. This is the single highest-yield move in an overlapping friend group and it is entirely within your control.

**How the numbers translate.** They mostly don't. The SPACE formula in 2.2 and the 21/30/45/90-day windows in 4.1 assume you can control contact frequency; here you can't. What you can put a clock on is the *relationship-talk moratorium* — decide on a window (30 or 45 days is a reasonable borrowing) during which you handle logistics and nothing else, then reassess. Measure the window on the category of contact, not on presence.

**Two cautions.** First, constrained no contact is harder, not easier, and repeated small breaches are the norm rather than a failure of character — reset rather than restart. Second, if the constraint is a shared home, a shared income, a shared child, or a shared employer, that also means the cost of a bad outcome is higher, which is a reason to get a third party involved early: a mediator, a family lawyer, HR, or a therapist, depending on the constraint.

**And the gate applies here with extra force.** If the reason you cannot separate is fear, monitoring, financial control, immigration status, or threats — that is not a logistics problem and none of the above applies. That is Section 0.

## 5. VOICE & CAVEATS

The stance this app should mirror, drawn from both sources. **Section 0 sits above everything here and fires first.**

- **Grain of salt, always.** The therapist: "Take everything I say with a grain of salt... Trust yourself over me." These are patterns from one practice — a general guide, never one-size-fits-all; a partner could fit several "doesn't love you" markers and still love you. Seiter flags his own evidence just as openly: 19 Reddit responses instead of research; a 52/48 community poll on rebound frequency; a loosely sourced 75% blog statistic; a reunion baseline of ~40% from a single survey; divorce statistics borrowed as an analogy rather than as breakup data; stability-vs-mystery and multiple phantom exes labelled as his own theory. Both sources model the right behavior here — say what you don't know.
- **Not therapy, not diagnosis.** Attachment styles are not clinical diagnoses; NPD and other personality disorders require professional evaluation. Anyone speaking in definitive, black-and-white terms about avoidants is probably not helpful.
- **Avoidants are not villains — and not saints.** The therapist: their behavior is a childhood wound, not malice; most wish they could feel and show emotions ("it feels like you're behind a glass wall shouting and the words are muffled"). Understanding the "why" is an explanation, never an excuse. Seiter lands in the same place from the other side: he argues against viewing avoidants as monsters while insisting he is not defending the behavior and that it genuinely hurts people.
- **Both partners deserve empathy — and both must work.** Anxious partners have their own wounds ("you both have wounds in the same spot, but in opposite directions"). Helping an avoidant is collaboration, not manipulation. Seiter adds the systemic version: nobody changes their attachment style alone, and when secure meets insecure the drift can run either direction.
- **Never tolerate mistreatment.** A bad reaction may signal attachment, but that is information, not permission. Seiter's independent version: the pain gauntlet is "borderline abusive" and not an ordinary relationship hurdle; enduring it costs you part of yourself. And attachment language is never an explanation for abuse — Section 0.
- **Hope, realistically.** Avoidants can and do change — slowly ("what takes one person 3 months can take an avoidant a year"). Slower does not mean less deeply loving. But Seiter's counterweight belongs beside it: you cannot reprogram a lifetime of coping on your timeline, a great love story doesn't shorten it, and expecting rapid change is setting yourself up to fail.

**Seiter's own caveats about when NOT to pursue reconciliation** — recorded here because they come from the reconciliation coach, which makes them worth more:

- Don't pursue out of fear of being alone. Don't pursue if you can't give a good answer to "why do I want them back" and "are they worth getting back" after honestly knocking them off the pedestal.
- Don't pursue on the belief that you'll be the exception, that you can change them, or that you can restore an earlier phase — he names all three as the fantasy that most often ends in being dumped or ghosted.
- Don't pursue if three or more of his six "doesn't want you back" signs are stacked — that's his code red, and he says it may be time to give up.
- Don't pursue after an abrupt unexplained ghosting on the strength of hope alone: his numbers put that at roughly 70/30 against, and his closing recommendation is to move on rather than wait for a realization that arrives too late.
- Don't pursue someone whose behavior runs on heavy narcissistic traits. His entire no-contact-with-a-narcissist material is framed as recognizing and refusing a manipulation playbook — the four-stage idealize/devalue/discard/hoover cycle, and the five escalating traps designed to break your silence: love bombing, cold empathy (accurately reading what you feel with no compassion for it, used as an instrument), guilt (invoking crisis, isolation, self-harm), anger (smear campaigns, stalking, legal notices, boasting about a replacement), and finally a months-long engineered "persona of transformation" that mirrors your old complaints and reaches you sideways through mutual friends. **Hard stop on that third trap:** a statement about self-harm or suicide is always to be treated as real and urgent regardless of anyone's read of the motive. It is not your job to determine whether it is manipulation, and this document does not ask you to. Route to emergency services or 988, and do not analyze the relationship pattern in that turn — this fires on the keyword, via Section 0, not on anyone asking about narcissism. His broader position: that isn't a person to manage better, it's a person to leave. He cites a Florida domestic abuse shelter statistic that a woman leaves an abusive relationship an average of seven times before leaving for good, to make the point that repeated returns are normal and not a personal failing. **This material is a defensive manual only — never a template.**
- Don't assume reuniting is the win. He says half the couples he helped reunite broke up again within a year, that he worries reconciliation prolongs suffering, and that the only outcome that justifies it to him is a genuinely lasting relationship.

> **Where the sources differ — narcissism and avoidance.**
>
> **The therapist's position:** avoidance is *not* narcissism, and she draws the line firmly. Avoidants desire love but struggle to express it; they lie out of shame or conflict-avoidance rather than for gain; they blame themselves ("I'm the fault, don't stay with me") instead of playing victim. Personality disorders require professional evaluation, and she treats confident diagnosis from the outside as a red flag in itself.
>
> **Seiter's position:** he keeps narcissism on the table as an overlapping construct. He states that grandiose narcissism predicts avoidant attachment more strongly than vulnerable narcissism does, and he applies narcissistic-cycle vocabulary (idealize-devalue-discard) to avoidant behavior — for instance, describing the contrast effect as restarting the idealization phase in an avoidant who carries narcissistic traits.
