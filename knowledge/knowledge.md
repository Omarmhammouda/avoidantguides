## 1. CORE MODEL

### 1.1 The wound and where it comes from
Avoidant attachment forms in childhood: emotions were treated as wrong, toughness and independence were demanded, and love was only secured by shutting feelings down (e.g., a parent who pulled away whenever the child showed emotion — effectively "I can't love you if you're too emotional"). Usefulness was valued instead of emotions, so acts of service became their love language. Many had strict parents where nothing was ever enough — fail and the parent is disappointed, succeed and approval is withheld — which is why avoidants can't self-acknowledge and become obsessed with work and money as visible proof "I am valuable." Many were actually very loving young children who were shunned for "caring too much," so when they finally attach, they love very strongly. Because the wound formed in infancy, their personality was shaped with it — "there is no person underneath that"; traits like hyper-independence may always remain (compromise territory), but the fear itself can shrink dramatically. Subtypes: **fearful avoidants (FA)** score highest on adverse childhood experiences, had to alternate between expressive and detached depending on a volatile caregiver, run hot-and-cold, and stay in unsatisfying relationships longer; **dismissive avoidants (DA)** learned detachment earns parental love, commit very slowly but then stick to one person. Trust template: anxious people trust others but not themselves; FAs trust neither; DAs trust themselves too much and others basically not at all. Avoidance is not narcissism: avoidants desire love but struggle to express it, lie out of shame or conflict-avoidance (not for gain), and blame themselves ("I'm the fault, don't stay with me") rather than playing victim.

### 1.2 Deactivation and the internal conflict
Deactivation strategies mute the avoidant's feelings — they set the symptoms of love dormant; it does not mean they're out of love. Deactivation triggers when emotions run high — which includes both more fighting AND more love/closeness, because subconsciously love equates to distance (that's how they secured parental love). Their commitment-fear doesn't feel like fear to them — it feels like refusal, stubbornness, "I just don't want to." They're not afraid of commitment itself but of what's *inside* it: losing space, autonomy, and identity; being controlled, manipulated, humiliated, judged; making the wrong decision and losing trust in themselves. The risks are real, but the ratio is off — they expect a 9 or 10 magnitude when it's really a 1. Detachment often has nothing to do with the partner (work stress, family issues); avoidants struggle to handle multiple things at once. They can leave people they love: they bond to the overall *feeling* around a person, so if they feel triggered, anxious, and cornered in the moment, they may go even while loving you. Almost all insecurely attached people (avoidants included) have some form of control issues; avoidants prefer to control the relationship's pace out of fear of being controlled and suffocated.

### 1.3 Shame, guilt, and failure
Failure is one of the avoidant's biggest triggers. After running from a relationship, their dominant feeling is: "relationships are hard, I made a mistake, you're never going to forgive me." Unhealed avoidants handle guilt by avoiding the topic or getting irritated (they're shutting down the "noise" of having disappointed you — "it's not you, but it does come out at you"), downplaying ("you're exaggerating" — denial precisely because it feels bad), making excuses, breaking promises to change once the guilt fades (more FA), rewriting your memory of events (a form of gaslighting, more FA), or — if the guilt is unmanageable — breaking up with you to escape it (more DA). Healing avoidants apologize genuinely without theatrics, explain for clarity while staying open to questions, show gradual visible change that doesn't revert, ask to be held accountable ("if you notice I haven't done it, tell me"), and later seek recognition of their improvement — which is itself a form of commitment. Many who "discard" genuinely believed disappearing would hurt you less than staying.

### 1.4 Bond vs chemistry
Passionate love is fast, electric, chemical — and it's the part avoidants can fake: many put on a charming, romantic front early precisely because they *don't* feel deep attachment yet (and are ashamed they struggle with love). Compassionate love — slower, steadier, consistent — is what "deep genuine love" means here, and it's the stage avoidants most value yet most tend to sabotage, because when authentic love starts, the fear starts. Genuine bond is different from chemistry: it's how you *know* each other — knowing how they act in a bad mood, small repair attempts after hurting you, giving each other space. Many avoidants fall in love because they feel you know something about them they never said in words. Depth can't be forced with anyone — they notice its absence when comparing conversations with others to conversations with you. Warning: fast early intimacy, promises, and commitment followed by collapse usually means they *don't* love you — a loving avoidant charms early but never commits early.

### 1.5 How avoidants fall in love
Avoidants vet slowly — like they buy houses and cars: long research, slow decision, then ride-or-die. Romance is a checklist to them (some literally state requirements on first dates); sudden commitment after years usually means the checklist quietly got fulfilled. They don't waste time on anyone who isn't valuable to them — "if an avoidant wants to give you breadcrumbs, they just might not even be there." They take action seriously, not words: love shows up as acts of service (changing your tire, airport pickups, packed meals), teaching you their hobbies, building you into their regimented schedule (if you're in their schedule, "you're pretty much here to stay"), consistency and reliability, silent sacrifices of comfort they never expect recognized, and blunt honesty. When truly attached they develop standards and expectations of you — this is reliance and vulnerability, not flaw-finding: "I want you to fix these things so we can have a long-term relationship." "Crashing out" with "you don't know how much I tolerate for you" is a badly-worded request for appreciation. They carry the concept of "the one" more than most styles; once committed they give their all — and caring how you view them is, to an avoidant, admitting they need you (which they were taught is weakness). They often don't know they love you until much later — sometimes too late.

### 1.6 Testing behavior
Avoidants test largely subconsciously — it's maladaptive coping, and unlike anxious testing (which seeks reactions to validate insecurity), avoidant testing is *vetting*: "are you valuable enough to invest in?" Concrete forms: saying no on purpose to see if you'll accept disappointment respectfully (the sweet spot is disappointed-but-respectful — many decide "she's the one" when you pass); declaring "I'll never get married / I can't give you what you want" (both a back-off move and a test — many later do exactly those things); deliberately delaying replies during good stretches (unhealthy — what they actually want is reassurance they're too proud to request); blunt criticism ("I don't enjoy your cooking") to see whether you'll judge and force them or hear them; offering feedback to gauge whether you can take it (they want an equal relationship); and sharing insecurities to test whether you'll side with them. When they come back after a breakup, they arrive with a testing agenda — let them run it; you don't come with one.
**How to respond when tested:** To "I can't give you what you want," answer with acceptance plus agency: "I know what I want. If I don't like you, I'll tell you and leave. You don't need to make that decision for me. You're not as bad as you think you are." If insecurity flares while they're away on a trip, raise it only *after* the trip, with self-located framing: "I was feeling insecure and need reassurance — I do believe you're loyal." When they offer blunt feedback, hear it out without denial. And when they share a family/childhood insecurity, explicitly side with them ("I don't agree with your dad — I like your makeup").

### 1.7 Placeholder vs "the one"
The opposite of love is not hate — it's indifference. Reactions, even negative ones (irritation, anger, shutting down, blame, arguments, endless expectations), are emotional investment; total nonchalance where nothing gets a rise out of them is the placeholder signal. If you're the one: they have lots of expectations of you (that's what they do with their forever things — they're picky about everything that matters); they ask for recognition ("do you not see how much I do for you?"); they know your moods, preferences, and standing orders in detail; their actions are stronger than their words (they resist verbal promises because breaking one means failing you, but real progress shows); and they slowly open up over time. Placeholder: no expectations, no recognition-seeking, forgets details a colleague would know, promises marriage/kids freely with zero action ("words stronger than actions"), lies just to not rock the boat, and no momentum. The one-year rule: avoidants don't stay with people they don't like — they'd rather be alone — so if they've stayed over a year, you're almost certainly not the placeholder. Caveat: FAs may lie to spare your feelings *because* they love you (abandonment wound), so the honesty test doesn't work on them.

### 1.8 What they need to feel safe
Acceptance is a core desire — accepting their timeline *is* accepting them ("if you can accept my timeline, you accept me"). They need: non-judgmental space (they're very sensitive to judgment); respect (no eye-rolling, sarcasm, mocking, comparing — rule of thumb: if you wouldn't text it to your boss, be careful texting it to your partner); consistency (not high text volume — knowing you're there); built-in space that never fully blurs; a partner who manages their own emotions at least some of the time; reassurance about their *specific* fears (manipulation, control, punishment for rescheduling — not "I'll never leave you"); appreciation of effort over outcome; a partner who lets go of little things; equals, not a pedestal (being pedestalized means "if I don't do what you want, you're going to scream at me"); and a partner with real self-esteem — flexible like a rubber band, not rigid ultimatums — because confident direct communication is the only language they hear (pouting, hinting, and withdrawing mean nothing to them, and prolonged confusion can push them out even when they care). Once they feel accepted with their original stance, they become far more willing to compromise — non-acceptance is the main barrier to avoidants changing.

## 2. SITUATION PLAYBOOKS

### 2.1 Pulling away / deactivation
**What's happening:** Usually not about you — it's childhood (they may even blame you and be wrong; "nobody knows themselves that well"). Closeness itself is their subconscious cue to shut down; pulling away after milestones (moving in, deepening intimacy) often happens while feelings are actually *deepening*. Often it's external stress they won't show. The "box" model: you fill the relationship box halfway waiting for them to match — but a full box feels heavy and suffocating to them.
**DO:** Become a little more independent (job, health, hobbies, friends) — it speaks their love-language of space; drop your "box" contribution to a quarter, let them fill a quarter, then build 10% at a time; communicate directly and logically ("You're canceling a lot, I don't like it — what can we do together to make this stop?"); recognize their acts of service as bids for connection; believe them when they say you didn't do anything.
**DON'T:** Blame yourself; smother with your own bids (compliments, constant availability — frequent compliments feel insincere and suffocating to them); read reduced replies as loss of love; interrogate mid-withdrawal.
**Expect:** With more space plus consistency, they typically re-approach — sometimes volunteering steps forward within weeks. Slow rebuild, not overnight.
**Intimacy withdrawal:** Pulling away from physical/bedroom intimacy is its own recurring case — a triggered fear response, "nothing to do with you," not lost attraction. Desire typically returns once they feel safe again, but it can take a year or more of patience (one patient pulled away sexually while privately wanting marriage). A workable tactic: verbally agree together to take breaks from high-pressure topics like bedroom intimacy — the agreed pause removes the pressure driving the shutdown.

### 2.2 Shutdown mid-conflict
**What's happening:** They weren't modeled how to process extreme feelings; your high emotion floods them and they can't hear anything. Continuing last night's argument today reads to them as a *second* fight ("this is overwhelming"). FAs may go silent for days. The emotion you see — irritation, anger, shutting down — is investment, not indifference. When you're upset they may literally be unable to concentrate (some can't drive or cook safely).
**DO:** Stay grounded; lead with logic that includes feeling; take accountability for your side *during* the argument (proves emotional balance and is what they later deeply regret losing); shelve non-pressing issues ("our relationship is overall good — back burner, let's do something fun"); bounce back the next day; frame it "me and you against the problem, not me versus you."
**DON'T:** Chase, corner, or give ultimatums (people with trauma communicate through ultimatums constantly — it produces fights and breakups); drag the fight across days; hold arguments against them later ("I remember when you did X"); treat their next-day normalcy as not caring — for them everything *is* fine, and that resilience stabilizes the relationship.
**Expect:** They often quietly start doing the disputed thing a couple weeks after the fight simmers down — delayed change is more permanent than immediate change.

### 2.3 Breakup & no contact
**What's happening:** Avoidants grieve the right person in stages: (1) numbness/detachment/even excitement ("I can finally be independent" — deactivation, not indifference; DAs stay here longer), (2) shock, (3) denial ("I never loved you" — protecting the belief that your emotions can't control them), (4) anger/distraction (FAs rebound into relationships they don't care about; DAs take trips, plan moves), (5) bargaining ("how are you?", "I'm sorry", "I miss you" — heavy hidden self-blame), (6) deep grief — the longest stage, never shown to you (DAs can silently stay here for years), (7) reflection, (8) acceptance, (9) growth. The initial "freedom" fades into emptiness — they see their life with your spot empty; independence stops tasting good ("the pepperoni pizza realization"). FAs feel the pain sooner; DAs take much longer to register it.
**DO:** Be yourself — including honestly admitting once, directly and respectfully, that you still want it ("I understand you don't want to be together. I still wish we could make it work, but I respect you") — then leave it; grieve openly and healthily; own your part of the breakup out loud; keep your life intact.
**DON'T:** Play games or perform indifference — they know you care, and pretending reads as inauthentic ("they can't appreciate something behind a wall"); beg or repeat your case; take the happy/detached phase at face value; treat a fast rebound as proof they've moved on.
**Expect:** If they discarded you over an external struggle, they'll very likely reach out once it resolves. Physical reminders of you in their space intensify the craving; suppressing the missing makes it stronger. Reaching out during deep grief is genuine — but taking them back is never your obligation.

### 2.4 They came back
**What's happening:** The main reason an avoidant ever comes back is that part of them wants to connect with you. They arrive testing the waters, often after their own failed self-work cycle ("I didn't do right, I'll try again"). Admissions like "I know I'm stubborn" took real courage — they're checking whether you'll accept them now that they have some awareness.
**DO:** Go slow — don't decide immediately, and if you've decided, don't say so; speak 20%, evaluate 80%; respond to admissions briefly ("That's true. I appreciate you being honest"); to "I miss you": "I missed you too, appreciate you sharing" — then verify it translates to action; keep it easy on apologies ("Water under the bridge"); look for new consistency in their life (job, school, friendships — life consistency carries into the relationship); evaluate bond over chemistry.
**DON'T:** Raise relationship issues right away — wait a couple of weeks of rapport, then small doses ("Is that still bothering you?"); pour out your hurt at the first meeting; overreact even positively to their vulnerability.
**Expect:** If you take acknowledgements calmly, they learn relationships aren't hard and become likelier to change. Chronic off-and-on cycles usually mean *two* unhealed people triggering each other — readjust expectations without compromising everything (exclusivity now, label later; they must meet you in the middle), or walk away without anger.

### 2.5 Blocked / ghosted / avoiding seeing you
**What's happening:** Many avoidants avoid in-person contact in some form: frequent canceling is mainly a control issue; avoiding weekends usually means they decompress alone after heavy work weeks (people fear cheating — a lot of the time it's not true); refusing one home or the other is often roommate/parent discomfort; sometimes they're still adjusting you into their life and can't admit committing means changing their lifestyle. They often deny how long it's been — denial about how severe their triggers are. After disappearing, unhealed avoidants frequently *want you* to reach out but won't do it themselves — afraid you're mad, afraid of disturbing your peace, afraid of rejection. Nuance: frequent long ghosting-and-returning cycles mean they're not serious about you or connection in general; and less commonly, someone avoiding seeing you is hiding another relationship.
**DO:** Lean into their proposed timing first (the weekday), build rapport, then extend toward the middle; raise cancellations constructively ("I appreciate our time, but the cancellations have me frustrated — is there a better time? I'm not mad, I just want to resolve this"); if they deny the timeline, use calm concrete evidence ("Last time we spoke was May 2025. It's been 12 months") and let them process the guilt — they need it to admit what's going on.
**DON'T:** Compromise on speaking up while being rigid about logistics — insecure attachment flips this exactly backwards; silently stew and act hurt instead of saying the thing.
**Expect:** They may crash out a little at the evidence — if they really care, they show up afterwards. The adjustment usually improves over months. The therapist explicitly does not tell you to stay: that's your decision.

### 2.6 Friendzone
**What's happening:** First check whether you're actually in it — if you're romantic, dating, or intimate long-term, you're something more; they just won't label it. Five barriers: (1) control/rigidity — keeping you a "friend" means no sprung expectations; it becomes a relationship when the pace feels like their own; (2) fear of disappointing you; (3) protecting the bond — "if the romance fails I lose you entirely" (more common with female avoidants); (4) external pressure — mostly co-workers (avoidants barely care what friends think, but do care about their job); (5) self-doubt about maintaining a romantic relationship — the barrier with the least push-back.
**DO:** Identify the barrier first, then match the method: for control — reciprocate, build rapport, prompt gently, accept plans modified to their terms; for disappointment-fear — communicate you don't hold high expectations and disappointment won't make you leave; for bond-protection — "no matter what happens between us, you'll never lose me, I'll always be in your corner"; for co-workers — a calm "I think there's something between us — how can we navigate this?"; for self-doubt — genuine reassurance of their strengths ("you're the first person I call").
**DON'T:** Demand a label or spring expectations; apply the wrong barrier's technique.
**Expect:** It works but depends on a lot; if they don't open up after stepping back and retrying, the relationship may not be for you.

### 2.7 Placeholder fears
**What's happening:** Avoidants are so subtle with love that many loved partners *feel* like placeholders. Run the six contrasts: honesty (DA: honest even when it hurts = respect; FA exception — may lie out of abandonment fear even when loving), conflict (emotion = investment; nonchalance = placeholder), recognition-seeking, awareness of your details, actions-vs-words on change, and slow self-disclosure. Add the timeline tests: over a year together = almost certainly not a placeholder ("95% of the time you're not the placeholder"); grand gestures with disappearing cycles = not serious; no progress at all after a year of consistent dating = not serious about you or about healing.
**DO:** Measure progress against *their own* baseline six months ago, not a normal standard; check your own side — are you making excuses for crumbs, or frustrated about a real connection with real (if insufficient) effort?
**DON'T:** Diagnose off one trait; read frustration ("nothing I do is ever enough for you?") as not caring — it's care poorly communicated.
**Expect:** If the signs genuinely point placeholder — no momentum, no expectations, no emotional reaction — free yourself from the dynamic.

### 2.8 Making them feel safe to commit
**What's happening:** Commitment hits nearly every avoidant fear at once (space, control, judgment, wrong decisions). They need reassurance about specific fears, acceptance of their timeline, and evidence you're trustworthy — chipped away slowly through experience.
**DO:** Build permanent space into the lifestyle (separate activities, friends, decompression time); reassure specifically while standing your ground ("If you reschedule, I won't crash out — as long as it's within a day or two, I'm okay"; "Moving in doesn't mean I'll suddenly demand marriage"); acknowledge effort out loud, however small ("You said you're not good at relationships — I've been with you three years; you are"); state feelings without demanding immediate change, then give it rest and revisit later (party topics after months, marriage after years), voicing appreciation for growth; conclude disagreements rather than cycling them; accept "possibly" as a real answer — tolerance of uncertainty makes them introduce more consistency; add no-pressure disclaimers ("If not, I understand and won't take it personally"); communicate your wants flexibly ("You know I want marriage one day; I'm flexible on timing, but I don't want it to become a topic we never discuss").
**DON'T:** Blur all boundaries; pressure or badger (people change by being accepted, not pressured); compare them to other couples; absorb triggered statements ("I'll never commit") as final truth.
**Expect:** A slower-than-secure timeline — you will not be married in a year or two; slow and steady wins the race, and slower doesn't mean less deeply loving. When ready to settle, watch for: back-seat decision-making, relaxed yeses to serious asks, unchanged anxious behaviors no longer triggering them, irritation voiced once and released, your perspective sought on big decisions, unwavering confidence about milestones.

### 2.9 Boundaries & self-respect
**What's happening:** The avoidant's partner is usually anxious-preoccupied or FA — low self-esteem, self-trust issues, abandonment wounds. Building self-esteem is the single variable that makes the avoidant lean in while you feel respected — and it's teamwork, not manipulation. Real self-esteem is a rubber band (flexible with a limit), not rigid ultimatums.
**DO:** Weigh reassurance requests before making them; regularly do things alone by choice; invest in your own career and future; speak directly and confidently instead of hinting from the backseat; state needs plainly without ultimatums ("I wish you could text me more often"); name patterns ("I noticed you're taking longer to reply — just let me know when you get a chance"); observe, don't absorb — don't let their moods take over your day; hold them accountable without contempt or repetition.
**DON'T:** Pout, withdraw, or shut down as communication — it means nothing to them and the confusion can push them out; put them on a pedestal or attach your self-worth to the relationship; bend over backwards for things nobody asked, then explode; use their bad reaction as a reason to tolerate mistreatment — attachment-signal or not, abuse is never something to accept.
**Expect:** Either they get more comfortable opening up, or you gain clarity to ask for more or walk away healthily. Warning to the avoidant side: a partner with rising self-esteem will expect respect, honesty, and consistency — and will leave if the avoidant doesn't also improve.

### 2.10 When to walk away
**What's happening:** Love is only one ingredient of a foundation. Some situations aren't attachment-repair situations: grand-gesture cycles with disappearing (possibly a personality disorder, more complex than attachment alone); zero progress after a year of consistent dating; frequent long ghosting; cheating without remorse or interest in its source (cheating is a moral and emotional-regulation issue, not an attachment issue); years of blaming you with no accountability (also check whether you're enabling it — it's often fixable in a couple of conversations); reaching out only for favors; total indifference to your hurt; zero effort over years (sometimes meaning they're not even avoidant — they just don't like you); growing *more* distant over years (loving avoidants grow closer).
**DO:** Re-evaluate whether readjusted expectations can make you happy; if not, walk away without anger and heal; remember you're not trapped — when relationships lack potential, both people typically move on and find happiness.
**DON'T:** Wait around making excuses for a relationship with no weight; stay to "prove" the bond — sticking around builds their bond, but the therapist is explicit she is not telling you to tolerate being treated wrong.
**Expect:** Walking away from a genuinely non-serious dynamic is how you get the love you deserve rather than emotional scarring.

### 2.11 Long-term relationship maintenance
**What's happening:** Long-term partners of avoidants share learnable traits, and avoidants name them in therapy: "she lets go of little things," "I trust their opinion when they call me out."
**DO:** Manage your emotions visibly; correct them respectfully and in private; value effort over outcome; let go of little things (momentarily, not permanently); give space but stay consistent ("I'll text you tomorrow" — then do); talk about yourself as an equal; introduce new shared experiences; be supportive of their hobbies rather than shunning them; share knowledge both ways (they light up when you add "logical evidence" for why they're with you); compromise creatively (they hate holidays? do a holiday-inspired date another day); recognize silent sacrifices out loud; discuss family time/money boundaries calmly (to them family support is duty-love); apologize outwardly for your own slips — they model it back; model curiosity and confidence about outcomes — it quietly teaches them they don't have to be rigid or controlling; speak positively about relationships in general (influence without pressure).
**DON'T:** Pedestalize them; trauma-bond or gossip as connection (avoidants find it off-putting); poke at newly-opened areas ("are you sure? you were mad about this last year" — it collapses the opening); repeat prompts to change (articles, quizzes — repetition is really annoying to them).
**Expect:** Small changes generalize — the therapist has never seen small changes appear elsewhere while the main issue never resolved. Avoidants normally grow closer over years; they're very giving once they open up — "once they give you all, they give you all."

### 2.12 Avoidant or just not interested?
**What's happening:** The most reliable discriminator is labels versus behaviors. An avoidant who loves you resists *labels* but displays *relationship behaviors*: exclusivity, regular contact, loyalty — and their commitment-resistance *decreases* as the connection deepens. Someone who's simply not interested shows the inversion: verbal love and commitment (easy "I love you"s, big promises, future talk) while their actions contradict the words. Nuance: a partner who rarely sees you in person yet opens up to you about their childhood is showing the avoidant pattern — a real bond plus contact-avoidance — not disinterest.
**DO:** Judge actions over words in both directions; track whether resistance is shrinking as trust builds.
**DON'T:** Apply avoidant-repair techniques (space, patience, timeline acceptance) to someone who isn't avoidant — they will not work on a person who simply doesn't like you.
**Expect:** If the relationship behaviors are absent and only words remain, this isn't an attachment problem — see 2.10.

### 2.13 Sudden discard
**What's happening:** Abrupt, no-warning discards have identifiable mechanisms: invisible internal struggles that build silently until they snap; felt inadequacy ("all I do is make you cry, you deserve better"); too much fighting; feeling forced to choose between themselves and you — early in healing they choose themselves; and, most of all, genuinely believing disappearing would hurt you less than staying. This runs on 1.2's mechanism: they bond to the overall *feeling* around a person, so if the moment feels triggered, anxious, and cornered, they can leave while still loving you.
**DO:** Read the discard as their internal state, not a verdict on your worth; grieve per 2.3.
**DON'T:** Assume you must have done something catastrophic — the struggle was usually invisible by design.
**Expect:** Discards driven by an external struggle (work, family, money) very often end in outreach once the struggle resolves.

## 3. SIGNS DICTIONARY

### Signs of genuine love / bond
- Notices tiny discomfort signals (a sound, a hand on your stomach → "are you okay?") → deep attachment
- Says "I know I'm difficult and I appreciate you understanding me," "You have my word" (and follows through), "You make me feel better," "I'm sorry" → the avoidant's slanted "I love you"
- "What can I do so you don't think that way about me?" → to an avoidant, caring how you view them = needing you; you're the one
- Exaggerated proving of innocence ("look through my phone," "I'll delete Instagram") → inexperience with a loved one thinking badly of them; usually genuinely innocent
- Has expectations of you, asks you to change specific things, nitpicks → reliance and long-term investment ("that's what they do with their forever things")
- "You don't know how much I tolerate for you" → request for appreciation, not rejection
- Mid-fight "if you think I'm so difficult, why are you even with me?" → a need for reassurance, not a wish to break up
- Blunt need-statements ("you're clingy," "stop asking me twice") → risking conflict to get needs met because they expect you long-term
- Shares day-to-day stressors flatly with no ask; shares shameful past; shares small mundane details → major vulnerability for an avoidant
- Detailed factual sharing about their day; talkative/dominating about hobbies → a DA opening up
- Follow-up questions about your day; updates from trips ("landed," "at the hotel") → moved past fear of looking needy
- Flexible with money around you; shares financial details → opening their autonomy: "I know you're never going to hurt me"

- Keeps/wears/uses items you gave them → indirect, safe treasuring
- Builds you into their regimented schedule (nightly 5-minute call, morning text) → "here to stay"
- Acts of service, protectiveness, teaching you their hobbies, sharing their stuff → their love languages

## 4. VOICE & CAVEATS

The channel's recurring stance, which this app should mirror:
- **Grain of salt, always.** "Take everything I say with a grain of salt... Trust yourself over me." These are patterns from one therapist's practice — a general guide, never one-size-fits-all. Every avoidant is different; a partner could fit several "doesn't love you" markers and still love you. You know your partner better than any video.
- **Not therapy, not diagnosis.** Attachment styles are not clinical diagnoses; NPD and other personality disorders require professional evaluation. Anyone speaking in definitive, black-and-white terms about avoidants ("avoidants always do X") is probably not helpful.
- **Avoidants are not villains.** Their behavior is a childhood wound, not malice — most wish they could feel and show emotions ("it feels like you're behind a glass wall shouting and the words are muffled"). Understanding the "why" is an explanation, never an excuse.
- **Both partners deserve empathy — and both must work.** Anxious partners have their own wounds ("you both have wounds in the same spot, but in opposite directions"); off-and-on cycles usually mean two unhealed people. Helping an avoidant is collaboration, not manipulation. But if your partner does no work at all, no technique may save it.
- **Never tolerate mistreatment.** A bad reaction may signal attachment, but that is information, not permission — abuse, chronic disrespect, or zero-effort dynamics are reasons to leave, and walking away without anger is a valid, healthy outcome.
- **Hope, realistically.** Avoidants can and do change — slowly ("what takes one person 3 months can take an avoidant a year") — and "when there's a will, there's a way typically." Slower does not mean less deeply loving.
